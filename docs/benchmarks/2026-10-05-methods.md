# Methods

## From a km tile to a seamless mosaic

A Berlin ALS tile is 1 km square and up to 27 million points (18 million on average; the
forest tiles 20-27 million); ForestFormer3D was trained on
plots of roughly 100 m. Each km tile is therefore split into a 10 x 10 grid of 100 m
sub-tiles, and every sub-tile is cut with a **20 m halo** of neighbouring points -- from
the same tile or, at the tile edge, from the adjacent km tiles -- so that a tree standing
on a sub-tile border is seen whole by at least one sub-tile. The model runs on each
haloed sub-tile; every point of the result is then traced back to its source, and each
point keeps the label of the sub-tile whose **core** (the 100 m square without halo) holds
it. Instances of neighbouring sub-tiles are matched on the halo points both have seen: two
instances that overlap with IoU ≥ 0.5 on those shared points are the same tree and get one
id. This stitch runs **once over the whole mosaic**, so ids are unique and dense across
every sub-tile and km-tile border; adding a tile means re-stitching everything, which is
why the mosaic is extended in batches.

The seam quality is measured on the result itself (`border-check`): the fraction of crowns
a 100 m grid line still cuts, and the excess of unlabelled points in a 2 m strip along the
grid lines over the interior. Shifting the measuring lattice by 50 m gives the control
floor those numbers are compared with (about 8 % of crowns touch any arbitrary line; 0 pp
excess).

## The methods

**ForestFormer3D** (Xiang et al. 2025) is a transformer over sparse-voxel features
(OneFormer3D lineage, SpConv U-Net backbone, 300 query points, one-to-many matching),
trained on the ForAINetV2 plots. It is used exactly as released, with cylinder crops of
16 m radius tiled over each sub-tile at a lattice pitch of 4 m (`region_step_factor`
0.25), 0.2 m voxels and a per-tile instance score threshold of 0.4. The production runs cost
about 1.5 GPU-hours per km tile on an H100 (49.6 GPU-hours for the first 33 tiles, and the
57-tile mosaic was reached in two further batches of eleven and thirteen tiles) -- a throughput
figure measured with five to seven queues sharing the host, not the speed of the method alone.
One process alone on an idle H100 (`benchmark/speed_benchmark.sh`, 2026-10-08, two tiles of
25.2 M and 15.1 M points) costs **414 s per million points**, i.e. 2.0 h for the mosaic's mean
tile, at 21-38 % GPU utilisation and under 4.2 GB of memory; SegmentAnyTree costs 164 s per
million points (0.8 h per mean tile, 24-28 % utilisation) and AMS3D 21-69 s per million points
on 48 cores, depending on how much vegetation it has to cluster. Both deep methods are linear
in the point count to the third digit, so the per-sub-tile seconds quoted elsewhere only
compare between tiles of similar density. That one process keeps an H100 a third busy is why
production puts several queues on a card -- and why the batching described next was worth
doing. Seven GPUs process 22 tiles overnight. The
per-cylinder loop was profiled and tightened in two steps after the first runs: the
vectorised z-filter (a per-mask Python loop with ~590 blocking GPU syncs per cylinder had
been 81 % of prediction time) and, after the mosaics reported here were produced, batching
of `region_batch` = 8 cylinders through the sparse collate, UNet and point heads
(inference step 73 s -> 54 s per 100 m sub-tile on an idle H100, flat beyond 8, results
unchanged within the run-to-run noise). The decoder and the mask post-processing still
run per cylinder, which is why a single process keeps an H100 only ~40 % busy and under
10 GB of memory; production ran several queues per card for that reason.

**SegmentAnyTree** (Wielgosz et al. 2024) is a PointGroup-based model trained on a mix of
TLS, MLS and ULS forest data; the released checkpoint is run from a containerised fork over
the same haloed sub-tiles, its output converted to the same per-point contract and merged
by the same stitch. It preserves the input point order, which makes the per-point
comparison exact; the production runs cost about 1.9 GPU-hours per km tile with two containers
sharing each GPU.

**AMS3D** is an in-house adaptive mean-shift crown segmentation (CPU only, no learned
weights): points are clustered by a kernel whose bandwidth grows with height above
ground, the classical approach the deep models are usually compared with. It runs
through the same sub-tiles and stitch; at 48 workers a km tile takes about 22 minutes
(four such queues share the 224-core host).

**PointTreeFormer** (Stefan Reder, HNEE) is a fourth segmentation whose results, not code, enter
this report: he ran his model over 15 km tiles of the Tegel block (2026-10-05), each with a 20 m
buffer, writing per-point ground/wood/leaf and instance ids per tile. `benchmark/ptf_to_ff3d.py`
crops the buffer, puts the points into our result LAS order (the core holds exactly the tile's
source points) and applies the 2 m rule, so per-point agreement is exact; its ids are not unified
across km lines, since it did not go through the mosaic stitch.

The first three therefore differ only in the segmentation step; the points, the halo, the core
ownership and the stitch are identical, so every per-point comparison is like for like, and
PointTreeFormer shares the points and the height rule but not the stitch.

## Products and post-processing

Per km tile and method: a LAS 1.4 point cloud with `treeID`, `semantic` (ground / wood /
leaf) and `score` per point; a tree table (`tree_id`, position, top height, height above
ground, crown area, point count, mean score); crown polygons; a 0.5 m instance raster
(the id of the highest point per cell) and a 0.5 m semantic raster; a report. Terrain
rasters (DTM 1 m, DSM and CHM 0.5 m) come from the same ALS.

**Minimum height.** Grass returns on meadows a few decimetres above the ground are
labelled *leaf* by a model that has only seen forest, and the instance head paves such
open ground with flat, wide "trees" in the lattice of the inference cylinders (on the
33-tile mosaic 13,522 ForestFormer3D instances were under 2 m, 1,716 of them with hulls
over 50 m², at a median score of 0.50 against 0.64 for all trees -- the score cannot
separate them, the height can). Since the 44-tile mosaic the stitch therefore drops every
instance shorter than **2 m** (top minus the ground grid at the stem, the tree table's
own height) before any table, raster or report is written; points keep their semantic,
ids are not renumbered. The same rule is applied to all three methods
(`ff3d_geo stitch --min-height 2`; `ff3d_geo filter` for an existing result). On the
57-tile mosaic it removed 21,447 instances, and no flat blob is left in the products: the
analytics chapter's flat-blob count is zero for every method.

The Berlin ALS classification has **no building class** (roof points sit in the vegetation
classes), so every method segments roofs and roof vegetation as trees. The building mask
step takes the official ALKIS footprints, buffers them by 1 m, re-labels every point
inside as *building* and drops an instance when at least half of its points are on a roof.

### Pre-inference mask

The building mask above runs *after* inference, which is why the raw model output in the
viewer shows trees on roofs at all, and why a jetty or a canopy that carries no building
footprint keeps its "trees". The alternative is to keep those points away from the model:
`ff3d_geo run --mask-polygons <gpkg[:layer]>... --mask-buffer 1.0` leaves every point
inside the buffered polygons out of the PLY that goes to the GPU, stores them in a
`<stem>_premask.npz` sidecar, and puts them back into the result LAS in source order as
*building* with `treeID -1` and score -1; the report gains a `premask` block with how many
points were held back. Three ALKIS layers are prepared for it (data-sources chapter):
building footprints, water (`alkis:tatsaechlichenutzungflaechen`, the AX_*gewaesser*
classes) and `alkis:bauwerkeflaechen` -- the Landebrücke, Überdachung, Carport and Brücke
polygons that cover exactly the jetties over the Tegeler See whose planks the model reads
as crowns. Removing the points before inference also removes the roof instance's influence
on its neighbours, which no post-hoc mask can undo, and it shortens inference a little.

**The mosaics in this report were produced without it** -- they carry the post-hoc mask
only. The pre-inference mask is implemented, tested and staged with its polygons; applying
it means re-running the 57 tiles, which is scheduled after the speed and batching work.

## References and metrics

*Agreement between methods* is computed per tile on identical points: instances of method
A and B are matched when their point sets overlap with IoU ≥ 0.5; reported are the
matched fractions, the median IoU of matches, and the *split* fractions -- the share of A's
trees whose points are covered by two or more B instances, and vice versa.

*Against the forest stand map*: every predicted tree is joined to the 2014 stand it stands
in; per stand the 90th percentile of predicted heights is compared with the inventory's
main-canopy height, by dominant species.

*Against the tree cadastre*: a cadastre tree counts as detected when a predicted tree top
lies within 3 m of its surveyed position (nearest only); for detected trees with a
cadastre height, bias, mean absolute error and correlation of the predicted height are
reported.

No per-tree ground truth exists inside the forest. The one hand-delineated, species-
labelled reference on these tiles -- the 974 WINMOL field-circle crowns -- is not yet used.

## Reproducibility

Every step is a tracked script in the repository (fork `cwinkelmann/ForestFormer3D`,
branch `fix/review-findings`): data fetch (`benchmark/fetch_berlin_*.py`), the per-GPU
queue and the mosaic-wide stitch (`benchmark/berlin_run_gpu.sh`, `berlin_stitch.sh`,
`berlin_extend_mosaic.sh`), the comparison methods (`sat_run_gpu.sh`, `ams3d_run_cpu.sh`),
the analytics (`berlin_analytics.py`), the QGIS project (`make_qgis_project.py`), the
viewer (`build_potree_site.py`) and this report (`build_report_pdf.py`). The GPU host is
an 8 x H100 server; the geospatial code is pure Python and runs on a laptop.
